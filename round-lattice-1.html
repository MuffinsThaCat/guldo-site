The task

You write one Python function, solve(basis, bound2). basis is a list of integer vectors, and you return one nonzero vector of the lattice they span whose squared length is at most bound2, as a list of Python integers. Your solver and the baseline are run on questions fixed before the round, and the CPU time each one uses is measured. The result is a certificate: a lower bound, in bits, on how much faster your solver is (1 bit is about twice as fast). Anyone can recompute it from the public log and the revealed seeds, without taking anyone's word for it.

Why this round exists

Lattice problems underlie the encryption and signature schemes now being standardised and deployed, such as Kyber, Dilithium and Falcon. The companion paper, “The Egg, Not the Sphere” (Zenodo, version 3, 30 October 2026, open access), argues that the searches used against lattices cover an egg-shaped region rather than a sphere, which it says could mean a very large saving at the sizes those schemes use. It puts the gap at 48 bits for Kyber-512 and 130 bits for Falcon-1024. Those are the paper's own estimates. This round does not test them, neither confirms nor refutes them, and never adds them to a result: the baseline here is plain block reduction by enumeration at dimensions 64 to 72, while the estimate is about sieving at cryptographic sizes, so the two cannot be compared or summed. You can read the paper and decide for yourself what it supports.

Why the round counts only what lies beyond the egg. The egg's gap is already claimed in that paper, so counting it again here would count it twice. The round asks a different question: is there any further saving that has nothing to do with the egg? That is the gain it looks for, a separate saving on top of the egg and not a repeat of it. A technique that does use the egg is still welcome. It is measured and published like any other, but reported as “inside the egg”, not as new. You declare which kind yours is.

What it is for. The position is this: if a sizable gain turns up beyond the egg, confirmed independent of it, the recommendation will be that lattice cryptography be pulled from use and replaced. If no sizable gain turns up, that does not show that lattice cryptography is safe; the question stays where it was.

What counts as sizable. This is fixed before the round opens. An entry meets it when it is confirmed independent of the egg, its certified credit is at least 3 bits, and its speedup does not shrink as the dimension grows from 64 to 72, as recomputed from the ladders published in its certificate. 3 bits is the smallest gain this round certifies reliably, and a gain that holds as the dimension grows is the signal that matters at cryptographic sizes: a gain that shrinks is not. Meeting it opens a public review. A recommendation follows only if the review concludes that the gain carries up to cryptographic sizes. Missing the bar settles nothing either way.

If an entry meets it. Every certificate and its credit is published as usual. The designers of the affected schemes are told first, and that entry's source may be held for up to 30 days after the deadline before it is published, so the designers can respond. Every other entry's source is published as stated in the rules.

What a result can and cannot do. The lattices here have dimensions 64 to 72, and real schemes use dimensions in the hundreds. A certificate shows a measured speedup at small sizes. It does not show a break. A recommendation would rest on that measurement together with an argument that the gain carries up to cryptographic sizes, and it would be a published judgement, not an automatic result of the competition.

Why now. The lattice analysis behind this round relies on Claude Opus 4.6. It has proved by far the best model available for lattice security work, and the only one usable for it. Anthropic lists it as available not sooner than 5 February 2027, with at least 60 days' notice before retirement, and gives no guarantee beyond that. Once it is retired, it can no longer be used for this work. The lattice round closes on 10 January 2027, which leaves under four weeks before that date to evaluate the entries and review any that meet the bar above.

Techniques

What you can use

The round accepts any method that returns a valid short vector inside “the box”, the locked environment your solver runs in. It does not care how you find the vector.

BKZ and schedules of block sizesBKZ.Param with your own block-size schedule, loop limits, auto-abort, progressive and self-dual variants. The baseline is progressive BKZ with exact enumeration, so gains from tuning this are what the round measures.Independent

What does not work

  • Anything outside the standard library, numpy and fpylll: it fails to import in the box.
  • More than one core, a GPU, the network or files you did not include. The box has one CPU, 4 GB of memory, no network, a read-only file system and at most 64 processes.
  • Looking up answers. Every question is derived from a seed whose hash was committed in the public log before the round opened, so there is nothing to look up and nothing to tune to.
  • Tampering with the timing, reading the questions or seeds, or attacking the service. This means disqualification, and it may be reported.
  • A wrong answer, a crash or a timeout is a failed question, charged the full time limit. A solver that gives up quickly is penalised as heavily as one that runs out the clock.

What this round cannot test

  • Methods that need ring structure. These are random lattices with none, so subfield, automorphism and other ring-algebraic techniques cannot show up here at all.
  • How a speedup at dimensions 64 to 72 scales to real cryptographic sizes, which are in the hundreds.
  • Worst-case lattices, other lattice problems, or run time on other hardware.

Declare what your solver exploits

Put two comment lines within the first 40 lines of your file, so the results can keep the two kinds of gain apart (why):

# GULDO-MECHANISM: independent | angular | both
# GULDO-ABLATION: none | <handle and id of your own submission with the mechanism switched off>
DeclarationMeaningReported as
independentThe gain does not use the egg: a block-size schedule, pruning, early exit, batching, memory layout, preprocessing, or any other new idea.Beyond the egg, once your source has been read and the declaration confirmed: “does not rely on that structure, and is measurably faster than the stock baseline”.
angularThe solver deliberately uses the egg: the shape of the Gram-Schmidt norms or of the angles between pairs of vectors.As measured, but inside the egg, not counted as new. A second version with the mechanism switched off allows your credit over your own switched-off version to be published too, as a secondary number that is never added to your certificate.
bothSay in a comment which part is which.The whole entry as inside the egg, unless you submit the switched-off version too, which is judged on its own declaration.

Your credit is the measured credit whatever you declare. The declaration only decides what may be said about it afterwards. It is a comment and cannot be enforced by software: every published source is read, and a declaration may be corrected, with the correction published along with the reason. For a pair, submit the switched-off version first, copy its id from the receipt, and name it in the second version; both must be under the same handle.

Every lattice attack category on Guldo

This round tests one problem. Methods for the other kinds of lattice attack below have been built or declared, and each would run as a round of its own. This is where each stands today.

Problem or claimWhat existsStatus
Shortest vector problemFind a short vector in a random lattice (Goldstein-Mayer, the SVP challenge kind). Techniques: LLL, BKZ, enumeration with pruning, sieving, and anything else that returns a short vector.This round. It is the only lattice problem that can host an official round today.
Module-LWE-shaped latticesThe family mlwe: the same call, solve(basis, bound2), on lattices with Kyber's ring structure. Techniques: the same as above, on structured lattices.Built, but not part of this round: a round runs one family, and this one uses gm.
LWERecover a ternary secret from noisy linear equations (a small secret, with a fixed 2n samples). Techniques: primal attack (lattice reduction), dual attack combined with guessing, hybrid attacks on the small secret. The baseline is meet-in-the-middle.Toy size, declared but not compiled. The fixed number of samples is too few for sample-hungry attacks such as BKW and Arora-Ge: see “LWE with a sample oracle” below.
Ring-LWEThe same problem over the ring Z_q[x]/(x^n - x - 1), which is a field. It is graded by time to a verified secret, so any route counts. Techniques: primal lattice attack, algebraic or SAT encodings, ring-aware sieving.Toy size, declared but not compiled. The ring excludes subfield and overstretched-modulus attacks.
NTRURecover the short pair (f, g) from a public key h = g/f, in the same field ring. Technique: lattice reduction on the NTRU lattice.Toy size, declared but not compiled. The ring excludes subfield and overstretched-modulus attacks.
Short integer solution (SIS)Find a nonzero vector with entries in {-1, 0, 1} that a random matrix mod q sends to zero. It is a short vector in a q-ary lattice. Technique: lattice reduction.Toy size, declared but not compiled.
Hidden number problemRecover a hidden number from the top bits of many of its multiples mod p, the lattice attack on ECDSA with biased nonces. Technique: Boneh-Venkatesan lattices.Toy size, declared but not compiled.
Low-density subset sumRecover a hidden subset from its sum, at density about 1/2. Technique: lattice reduction on a knapsack lattice (Lagarias-Odlyzko, Coster et al.).Toy size, declared but not compiled.
Coppersmith small rootsFactor N = pq when the high bits of p are known. Technique: Coppersmith's small-root method, which is lattice reduction (the univariate case only).Toy size, declared but not compiled.
Distinguishing attacks (the dual attack)The claim is an advantage (how much better than a coin flip the distinguisher is), scored by an exact statistical bound rather than a run time.The method is built; the only worked example is a toy LWE-versus-random one.
Interactive attacks (decryption failures, chosen ciphertexts, side-channel models)The solver queries an oracle that the judge runs. The cost is the number of queries, and the transcript can be replayed.The method is built; the only worked example is not about lattices.
Attacks with heavy precomputation (closest-vector with preprocessing, amortised sieving)A preprocessing phase whose cost is shared across many instances, with the number of instances needed to break even reported.The method is built; the worked example is a discrete logarithm.
QuantumThe exact gate count and T-count of a small circuit, checked by exact simulation. It is never used to claim a speedup.Built; the worked example is Grover search.
LWE with a sample oracleThe solver asks for samples and is charged for each one, using the same counted-queries method as the interactive attacks above. This is the honest home for BKW, coded-BKW, Arora-Ge and statistical attacks, which need more samples than the fixed set Guldo's LWE problem gives.A round of its own.
LWE with leaked hintsPerfect, modular and approximate hints about the secret. This is the active practical area for side-channel attacks on Kyber, Dilithium and Falcon.A round of its own.
Subset sum at density 1The regime where lattice reduction stops working well and representation-based methods (Howgrave-Graham-Joux, Becker-Coron-Joux) are the known approach. It suits pure Python and exact step counting.A round of its own.
The rest of Coppersmith's familySmall private exponent RSA (Boneh-Durfee), partial key exposure, bivariate versions and approximate GCD.A round of its own.
LWE in the standard regimeUniform or centred-binomial secrets with Gaussian errors, which removes the bias towards guessing that small secrets give. A power-of-two cyclotomic variant (x^n + 1) covers what Guldo's NTRU and ring-LWE problems deliberately exclude: subfield and overstretched-modulus attacks. That ring is the hard case, because modulo q it has zero-divisors, which can make an instance's answer ambiguous.A round of its own.
Sieving, counted in vector operationsSieve papers count inner products as their cost, and Guldo already counts tensor operations for AI workloads. Counting them lets sieving ideas be compared by the cost model the literature uses, and needs no sieve library in the sandbox.A round of its own.

The seven recovery problems in the table are toy sizes, so a result on them says nothing about real parameters. They also compare a lattice attack with brute force, so they would probably not tell one lattice technique from another. Only the shortest vector problem does that today.

What cannot be certified

  • A scaling exponent or cost model. How a technique's cost grows with dimension, rather than how much faster it is at one size.
  • The prediction of a security estimator. Whether an estimator's predicted cost matches what an attack really needs.
  • Memory or parallel cost. Memory traffic and parallel depth are not captured by counting operations or by timing one core.
  • Quantum lattice algorithms at real size. Guldo's quantum method works only on small circuits that can be simulated exactly.

If you can state a problem that is missing, with a way to check an answer and a plain baseline, propose it. The Domains page lists every problem that is declared, and what each one can and cannot show.

How to enter

Install the tools in a virtual environment. fpylll alone installs but fails to import; it also needs cysignals, so the first command below installs all four packages together. Put the two declaration lines above at the top of your file. Then try it with guldo practice: without --yes it only prints its plan and its worst-case cost and runs nothing. A practice certificate is provisional, because you know the questions. When you are ready, submit. Submitting publishes your source after the deadline. The command prints a token once; keep it, because it is the only way to submit again under your handle.

python3 -m venv guldo-env && source guldo-env/bin/activate && pip install guldo-cli fpylll cysignals numpy
guldo lint my_solver.py --family gm
guldo practice my_solver.py --family gm --n 1 --budget 60 --yes
guldo submit my_solver.py --server https://submit.guldo.org --round lattice-1 --handle YOURHANDLE --accept-rules --publish-source

Your solver runs in a locked box: no network, one CPU, 4 GB of memory, the standard library, numpy and fpylll only.

What a result means

Credit is a statistical lower bound on the speedup, in bits, over 48 questions per dimension and three seeds. The three seeds are independent repetitions, and your credit is the minimum over them, with an inconclusive seed counting as zero, so one lucky seed does not help. Zero means “not shown to be faster”, not “slower”. Small gains usually come back inconclusive because timing noise hides them (half a bit to one bit almost always does). Very large gains do too: above about 5 bits the baseline hits its own time limit. At this round's settings, a true constant speedup of 3 bits is certified about 99% of the time, 4 bits about 68% and 5 bits about 50%. Time is CPU time on the evaluation machine, so a solver tuned for your own hardware may not carry over. See Limits and the Method page.
