Certify

Measure a result. Prove it to anyone.

Not here to compete. You have a solver, an attack, or a benchmark and you want a verifiable measurement that anyone can check independently. No round, no opponent, no deadline.

One command

Run your solver on three secret seeds of your own. The tool measures the cost, checks every answer, and writes a certificate: one file that carries the claim, the rules, the seeds and every raw measurement.

guldo practice my_solver.py --family gm

For exact instruction counts instead of seconds (reproducible on any machine with the same Python version):

guldo practice my_solver.py --family factor --count --dims 38 40 42

Without --yes each command only prints its plan and what it would cost; nothing runs. A lattice run can take hours and needs pip install "guldo-cli[lattice]".

The certificate is a JSON file. It is self-contained: anyone can check its arithmetic and fairness without the operator, a server or this site. What a certificate cannot show by itself is that the timings are true. You took them, so they are your claim; see “What it proves” below.

Anyone can check it

Hand the certificate to a reviewer, a standards body, or a journal. They verify it on their own machine with one command: no account, no server, and no need to take the sender's word for what verification covers (the list below says what it does not).

guldo verify their.cert.json

Checking never runs any code from the certificate. It verifies the log chain, checks that the seed commitments came first, confirms the certificate holds exactly the instances those seeds produce, and re-derives the credit from the raw timing records with the verifier's own copy of the protocol code.

Others can attest

An independent party can go further: re-run a sample of instances on their own hardware and sign the outcome. Their attestation is a separate file anyone can check.

guldo attest sign x.cert.json --key my.key --rerun 5 --execute --out x.my.att.json
guldo attest check x.cert.json a1.att.json a2.att.json

An attestation is worth exactly what the signer's identity is worth: one signed by a university lab you know means more than one signed by an unknown key.The tool separates attestations from keys you chose to trust from ones you did not. --execute runs the certificate's solver on your machine with no protection: do it in a throwaway virtual machine.

Put it on the record

A certificate proves itself to whoever holds the file. To make it findable and permanent, a log operator can verify it and add it to the public certificate log: an append-only, witnessed list you can prove a certificate is in, and from which anyone can recompute claims like “nothing submitted here reaches T bits.” Being in the log is not an official result, and not being in it does not mean a result does not exist.

Use cases

  • Lattice security. Record a lattice attack's cost across dimensions in a form others can check. What anyone can verify is that the instances are the ones the seeds produce and that the credit follows from the recorded costs. The costs themselves are your measurement until a step-counted run is re-executed, or someone independent re-measures it and signs.
  • Algorithm benchmarks. Compare your solver with a baseline on instances generated from seeds, with every answer checked. With seeds you chose yourself you could have tuned to them; a round's seeds are committed before submissions open, which is what rules that out.
  • Reproducible research. Attach a certificate to a paper. A reviewer can check its arithmetic and fairness without re-running anything, and can re-run a sample to test the timings themselves.
  • AI model evaluation. Measure inference quality or operation counts on committed instances. See AI workloads for what each kind of evidence shows.

What it proves and what it does not

A practice certificate uses your own seeds and your own machine. That means:

  • Proven: the instances are the ones the seeds produce; the solver answered them; the credit follows from the recorded costs; the protocol code is identified by hash.
  • Not proven: that the timings are honest (you ran it). Step counting (pure Python, Python 3.12 or newer) makes costs exactly reproducible, but only for someone who actually re-executes them (guldo cert verify X --rerun 5 --execute, in a throwaway virtual machine): verification alone does not, and the counter cannot see work done inside built-in C functions. For timed runs, independent attestations close the gap only as far as you trust the people who signed them.
  • Not proven: that the seeds were committed before you saw them (they are yours). In a competition round, the seeds are committed publicly before the deadline. A practice certificate is weaker on this axis, and says so.

The certificate itself states whether it was produced in a round or standalone, whether costs are seconds or counts, and whether the solver source is included. The verifier reports all of this. Full limits.
